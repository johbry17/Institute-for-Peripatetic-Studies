Field Notes — No. 1

The Epistemology of the Unnecessary Detour

Institute for Peripatetic Studies  ·  Autumn 2024

Available

Abstract. A preliminary examination of what, precisely, is learned by taking a route that was not required. The paper addresses whether knowledge acquired incidentally — during a deviation, a digression, or a return by an unfamiliar path — differs in kind from knowledge sought directly, and whether the difference, if any, matters to the conduct of peripatetic inquiry.

I. The Problem

There is a category of knowledge that no one sets out to acquire. It arrives incidentally, during the intervals between intended activities: the unexpected view from a corner turned too early, the neighborhood discovered while finding a way around a closed road, the character of a district encountered during the return rather than the outward journey. This knowledge was not sought. It was, in a precise sense, unnecessary — the intended destination could have been reached without it.

The question this paper addresses is whether such knowledge is, as a consequence of its incidental acquisition, somehow less reliable, less representative, or less useful than knowledge acquired through a deliberate and planned inquiry. The Institute's provisional answer is that it is not. The more interesting question is why not, and whether the answer requires us to revise our understanding of what directed inquiry is for.

II. What the Detour Reveals

Consider the conditions of the necessary journey: the destination is known, the route is at least partially determined, and the observer's attention is organized around the goal of arrival. Under these conditions, the landscape between origin and destination serves primarily as interval — it is the space through which one must pass before the inquiry can begin. Observation occurs, but it is subordinated to the logic of the route.

The unnecessary detour disrupts this logic. By definition, the detour has no instrumental justification: it does not reduce journey time, bring one nearer the destination, or fulfill any practical function. What it does — and what appears to be its characteristic epistemological contribution — is to remove the observer from the context in which the landscape is expected to be a certain way.

The detour places the walker in terrain that was not anticipated. This condition of mild surprise — of encountering something for which no interpretive framework was ready — appears to produce a particular quality of attention.

This is not the focused attention of directed inquiry. It is, rather, the broader, less structured attention of the person who does not know quite what they are looking for. The Institute proposes that this variety of attention, while less efficient than its directed counterpart, is sensitive to a different range of phenomena — specifically, to the features of a place that directed inquiry would not have thought to look for.

III. The Objection from Reliability

A reasonable objection at this point is that incidentally acquired knowledge is less reliable than deliberately sought knowledge precisely because it is not accompanied by the methodological controls that directed inquiry imposes. The researcher who sets out to study a district brings hypotheses, instruments, and a framework for interpreting what is found. The person who stumbles across the same district on the wrong side of a detour brings none of these things.

The objection is well-founded as far as it goes. The Institute does not claim that incidental observation is equivalent to systematic study, or that a single inadvertent glimpse produces the same quality of knowledge as a sustained investigation. What it claims is more modest: that the glimpse produces knowledge that the investigation, conducted from within its own framework, may not produce at all.

The methodological controls that make directed inquiry reliable also make it selective. A study designed to answer a specific question will be sensitive to evidence relevant to that question and relatively insensitive to evidence relevant to questions it was not designed to ask. The detour, which brings no predetermined questions to the landscape, may therefore register evidence that the directed inquiry cannot see — not because that evidence is hidden, but because it was not looked for.

IV. Knowledge and the Route Not Required

The Institute's provisional conclusion is that unnecessary detours do not produce a lesser variety of knowledge than directed inquiry. They produce a different variety — one that is, by its nature, not available through any route that was planned in advance. This does not mean that detours should replace directed inquiry; it means that they are complementary to it in a specific and valuable way.

The practical implication for peripatetic research is modest but significant: the planned route should not be regarded as the only legitimate source of observation. What is discovered on the way to somewhere else, or on the return by a different path, or during the departure from the established course, is not a supplement to the work. It is part of it.

Solvitur ambulando. The problem is solved by walking. The Institute proposes an addendum: it is frequently solved by walking somewhere one did not intend to go.

V. On Recording Incidental Observations

A subsidiary question concerns how observations made during unnecessary detours should be recorded. The field note tradition favors systematic recording: the date, the location, the conditions, the observation, and its potential significance. This is good practice, and the Institute endorses it.

The difficulty is that incidental observations rarely present themselves in a form amenable to systematic recording. They arrive as impressions, as incongruities, as a sense that something about a corner or a building or a piece of street furniture is not quite consistent with its surroundings — a sense that requires time and subsequent reflection to articulate. The field note written on the spot may capture only the surface of the observation. The fuller account emerges later, from memory and from the additional material that accumulates during the remainder of the walk.

The Institute therefore recommends a two-stage recording practice for observations made during detours and other forms of incidental inquiry: a brief note at the time of observation, recording location and the bare fact of attention having been drawn to something, and a fuller account written on return, once the observation has had the opportunity to settle and reveal its implications. Neither stage is optional.
