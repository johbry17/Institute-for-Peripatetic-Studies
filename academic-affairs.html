Academic Affairs

Distinctions conferred by the Institute

Academic Framework

The Institute confers two ceremonial academic distinctions upon persons it judges to have demonstrated a meaningful commitment to peripatetic inquiry.

These distinctions are self-created, self-conferred, and entirely non-accredited by any external body. The Institute considers this an asset rather than a limitation. Institutional authority, like most things of value, is ultimately a matter of conviction, ceremony, and sufficiently impressive paperwork.

The Institute's degrees are issued with complete sincerity and should be received in the same spirit. They do not qualify the holder for employment, graduate study, or professional licensure. They do qualify the holder for an enhanced sense of intellectual purpose when walking somewhere interesting.

Doctor of Peripatetic Studies

D.P.S.

Doctor of Peripatetic Studies

The Doctor of Peripatetic Studies is the highest academic distinction established by the Institute. It is awarded in recognition of foundational or exceptional contributions to the study of place through movement: sustained engagement with landscapes, substantive intellectual contributions to peripatetic inquiry, and demonstrated commitment to the methods and principles the Institute holds dear.

The degree was first conferred upon Bryan Johns, Founding Director, at the establishment of the Institute in Anno Domini MMXXIV. It was the natural and indeed only appropriate beginning for an institution of this kind.

Licentiate of Peripatetic Studies

L.P.S.

Licentiate of Peripatetic Studies

The Licentiate of Peripatetic Studies is the Institute's lesser — though by no means insignificant — ceremonial qualification. It is awarded to those who, in the judgment of the Institute and upon no more evidence than they themselves provide, have engaged in meaningful peripatetic activity and stand ready to continue doing so.

The Licentiate is conferred by diploma and accompanied by a formal letter from the Founding Director. Both documents are generated through the Institute's diploma generator and may be printed and displayed without embarrassment.

The Institute takes the view that a qualification earnestly received is worth as much as the earnestness of its reception. It therefore encourages all Licentiates to accept their distinction with the seriousness it deserves, frame it if they wish, and go for a walk.

Academic Regulations

The following regulations govern the conduct and obligations of those who hold distinctions conferred by the Institute. They are brief by intention.

  1. Reg. I

    No minimum distance of travel shall be prescribed. The Institute takes the view that the quality of attention brought to a walk is a more reliable indicator of peripatetic inquiry than its length, and that a thirty-minute excursion conducted with genuine curiosity is preferable to a ten-mile march conducted without it.

  2. Reg. II

    All Licentiates and Doctors of the Institute are encouraged to maintain an independent relationship with their intended route. Deviation from a planned course, whether deliberate or circumstantial, shall not be treated as a failure to complete the excursion, provided that observations have been made and honestly recorded.

  3. Reg. III

    Observations shall be recorded with due attention to uncertainty, context, and the limitations of the observer. The Institute does not require formal field notes, but it does require that the difference between what was seen and what was inferred be kept in view, if only privately.

  4. Reg. IV

    A holder of any Institute distinction who has walked somewhere interesting without arriving where intended should nevertheless consider the journey complete. Destinations are, in the Institute's considered view, starting conditions rather than measures of success.

  5. Reg. V

    Further distinctions within the Institute's system may be established at any time, at the sole discretion of the Founding Director, when a sufficiently persuasive intellectual or institutional necessity has been demonstrated. The Institute acknowledges that it is also the sole judge of what constitutes such a necessity.

Classification of Excursions

The Institute maintains the following provisional taxonomy of excursion types, offered as a reference for field note-keeping and as a supplement to the more general account of peripatetic method provided in the founding charter. The classification is not exhaustive. New categories will be established when the existing categories prove inadequate to describe what actually happened.

  1. I.

    The Contemplative Walk

    An excursion undertaken for the purpose of reflection, observation, or the gradual clarification of an unresolved question. The destination, if any exists, is secondary to the quality of attention brought to the journey. This category includes walks undertaken with no stated purpose, provided they are conducted with an appropriate degree of curiosity.

  2. II.

    The Errand That Became an Expedition

    An excursion that originated in a practical necessity — a journey to a specific address, an appointment to be kept, a parcel to be collected — and acquired, through the operation of curiosity or circumstance, a substantially greater scope than originally intended. Distinguished from the Deliberate Detour by the absence of advance intention.

  3. III.

    The Deliberate Detour

    An excursion in which a deviation from the intended route is undertaken with full awareness and without regret. Distinguished from error by its purposefulness, and from the Contemplative Walk by the existence of an original route that is being consciously departed from. The detour may be motivated by curiosity, aesthetics, a promising side street, or the desire to avoid a section of route already well-known.

  4. IV.

    The Unplanned Return

    An excursion that concludes by a route other than the one by which it began, whether by necessity, preference, or the gradual recognition that the original route has ceased to be interesting. The Institute regards the unplanned return as one of the more reliable sources of fresh observation, since the return route is typically encountered in a state of mild surprise.

  5. V.

    The Excursion Whose Destination Proves Less Interesting Than Its Route

    An excursion in which the intended terminal point is reached, inspected, and found to yield considerably less than the journey that preceded it. The Institute does not regard this outcome as a failure. It is, in fact, one of the more instructive discoveries available to the peripatetic researcher, and several of the Institute's foundational observations were made under precisely these conditions.

  6. VI.

    The Walk Undertaken to Verify an Observation

    An excursion motivated by a prior claim, hypothesis, or half-formed impression that requires confirmation in the field. The Institute notes that such excursions frequently produce evidence inconsistent with the original proposition, and that this inconsistency is to be recorded honestly rather than resolved by selecting a different route.

  7. VII.

    The Journey Interrupted by an Unresolved Question

    An excursion suspended, either temporarily or permanently, at a point of genuine intellectual difficulty. The interruption may arise because the question encountered resists easy resolution, because the observer has determined that the question is more interesting than the original destination, or because the conditions for continuing the excursion have ceased to obtain. Completion of the original route is neither expected nor required.

Founding Charter

The Institute was constituted by a founding charter, signed by the Founding Director at the time of the Institute's establishment. The charter sets out the Institute's name, mission, philosophical principles, governance structure, and authority to confer its ceremonial distinctions.

The charter is available for review and may be saved as a PDF through the browser's native print function.
