The Institute's Official Position on Detours

A Formal Statement of Conceptual Distinctions

In the interest of clarity, and in response to the absence of any existing institutional framework adequate to the subject, the Institute for Peripatetic Studies hereby sets out its official position on the status of detours in peripatetic inquiry. The detour has been insufficiently theorised. This declaration is a first step toward remedying that omission.

§ 1 — The Accidental Deviation

An accidental deviation is a departure from the intended route caused by error, misnavigation, inattention, or external obstruction. The walker did not intend to take this route; the deviation is, in the ordinary sense of the word, a mistake.

The Institute's position is that the accidental deviation is nonetheless a legitimate peripatetic event, provided that the walker, having recognised the deviation, chooses to observe their surroundings rather than merely to correct the error. The quality of the observation that follows transforms the accidental deviation into something more deliberate in its conduct, if not in its origin. The deviation is not retroactively intended; but it may be retroactively made useful.

§ 2 — The Deliberate Detour

A deliberate detour is a conscious departure from the intended route, taken for reasons the walker is prepared to articulate — if only to themselves. These reasons may include curiosity about what lies beyond the planned path, the desire to avoid a section of route already well-known, the appeal of a particular street or view, or a preference for a longer way that is, in some respect, better.

The deliberate detour is, in the Institute's view, a characteristic instrument of peripatetic inquiry. It requires a degree of independence from the original plan and a willingness to accept that the planned route is not the only route — that the map is an approximation rather than a mandate. The Institute regards these dispositions as scholarly virtues.

It is worth noting that the deliberate detour requires, for its definition, the existence of a route from which to depart. A walk with no intended route cannot produce a detour in the strict sense, though it may produce observations of equal or greater interest. The Institute treats this as a terminological rather than a substantive point.

§ 3 — The Route Whose Value Becomes Apparent Only Retrospectively

There is a third category, distinct from both the accidental deviation and the deliberate detour, which the Institute regards as among the more interesting forms of peripatetic experience. This is the route that, at the time it was taken, seemed neither particularly deliberate nor particularly accidental — a route chosen for no strong reason, or by default, or simply because it was there — and which reveals its significance only when the walker has returned, reviewed their notes, and recognised that something important was observed.

The Institute holds that this category cannot be planned for. It arises from the combination of sustained attention during the walk and the willingness to review observations afterward without the assumption that they have already yielded whatever they contain. It is among the strongest arguments the Institute knows for keeping field notes.

§ 4 — The Institute's General Position

The Institute's position, in summary, is as follows: a detour of any variety — accidental, deliberate, or merely retrospectively significant — is not a failure to follow a route. It is an encounter with the landscape that the route would not have produced. The landscape encountered during a detour is not less real, less interesting, or less worthy of study than the landscape that lay along the intended path. It is simply different.

The Institute further holds that the category of detour and the category of route are not as distinct as they may appear. Every walk involves decisions about which way to go. Some of those decisions are made in advance; others are made on the pavement, in conditions of partial information, under the influence of whatever is visible from where one happens to be standing. The deliberate detour and the planned route share more than they differ, and the distinction between them is largely one of timing.

Solvitur ambulando.
