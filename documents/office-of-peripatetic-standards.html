Office of Peripatetic Standards

On the Qualification of Legitimate Excursions

Whereas the Institute for Peripatetic Studies maintains a commitment to the serious study of place through movement, and whereas this commitment requires some clarity about what kinds of movement qualify as peripatetic inquiry, the following standards are hereby established. They are offered not as restrictions on the freedom of the walker but as a framework within which the Institute may recognize and record the work of its practitioners with appropriate precision.

§ 1 — On the Question of Intention

An excursion qualifies as peripatetic inquiry when it is undertaken with the intention of attending to the world encountered during its course. This intention need not be formulated in advance; it may arise during the walk itself, in response to something observed. What is required is that the walker, at some point during the excursion, is genuinely attending to where they are — not merely passing through it.

The Institute does not require that attention be sustained continuously throughout the walk. Lapses are normal and do not disqualify an excursion. What matters is that the capacity for observation is exercised at least occasionally and that whatever is observed is taken seriously.

§ 2 — On the Role of Observation

Observation is the primary instrument of peripatetic inquiry. It encompasses the full range of sensory and intellectual activity available to a person moving through a landscape: looking, listening, noting incongruities, making comparisons, asking questions that arise from direct encounter, and acknowledging what is unexpected.

Observation does not require equipment, specialist training, or a predetermined object of study. It requires, principally, the disposition to notice things and the honesty to record them as they appeared rather than as they were expected to appear. Where these conditions are met, the observation qualifies.

§ 3 — On Deviation from a Planned Route

Deviation from a planned route does not disqualify an excursion. On the contrary, the Institute takes the view that deviation frequently constitutes one of the more productive features of peripatetic inquiry, since an unplanned route places the walker in circumstances that directed inquiry could not have anticipated. The standards established here apply equally to the intended path and to whatever path was actually taken.

Where an excursion consists entirely of deviation — where no planned route existed, or where the original route was abandoned at the outset — it qualifies provided that the conditions of intention and observation described above are met.

§ 4 — On the Excursion That Fails to Arrive

An excursion that fails to reach its intended destination may nonetheless qualify as peripatetic inquiry in full, provided that observations were made along the route actually taken. The Institute does not regard arrival as a criterion of success. What matters is the quality of attention exercised during the journey, not the correspondence between the journey and any prior plan.

The failure to arrive may itself constitute a significant observation. An intended destination that proves unreachable, inaccessible, or absent from the landscape in the form expected is an object of inquiry in its own right, and should be recorded as such.

§ 5 — On the Minimum Conditions

The minimum conditions for an excursion to qualify as peripatetic inquiry are as follows: the walker was physically present in a landscape; they attended to some feature of that landscape with genuine curiosity; and they are prepared to give some account — however brief and however informal — of what they observed.

The Institute acknowledges that these conditions are not demanding. This is intentional. Peripatetic inquiry is not a scarce activity. It is a common one that lacks, in most cases, only the recognition it deserves.
